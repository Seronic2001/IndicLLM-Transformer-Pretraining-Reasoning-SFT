{\normalsize Consolidated Final Report (Phases 1, 2 and 3)}\\[8pt]
Shubhadeep Mandal \; (Roll No.\ 2025201056)\\
\small Branch \texttt{phase-3} \,\textbullet\, Language Models and Agents, Monsoon 2026\\[2pt]
\footnotesize Higher-resource model H: Hindi (Devanagari) \,\textbullet\, Lower-resource model L: Assamese (Eastern Nagari, \indic{অসমীয়া})
\end{center}
\vspace{-2pt}

\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
We built two independent monolingual pipelines, from corpus collection to reasoning fine-tuning, for Hindi and Assamese. Each language has its own 16,384-piece BPE tokenizer (fertility 1.28 and 1.43 tokens per word, no unknown tokens) and two 25M-parameter decoder-only Transformers written from PyTorch primitives: a Baseline V1 (learned positions, GELU, LayerNorm) and a Modern V2 (RoPE, SwiGLU, RMSNorm), each pretrained on 500M tokens. V2 reaches lower held-out perplexity in both languages (Hindi 36.15 vs 38.66; Assamese 51.55 vs 54.53 on 512-token windows), a 5--7\% advantage. Fine-tuned on synthetic relational reasoning (44 runs, 2 seeds per recipe), the models learn the task and generalise to chains up to three times longer than any in training: Hindi V1 with Direct SFT scores 0.961 on the test set and 0.846 on 10-hop chains, against a majority baseline of 0.48. A leakage analysis shows the main test is structurally covered by training (a lookup of training answers scores 0.95), so the evidence of reasoning comes from deeper, structurally new splits where that lookup is at chance. Direct SFT beats Chain-of-Thought SFT, V1 beats V2 despite its higher perplexity, and Assamese trails Hindi (best 0.833). Every model is misled by premises about a different attribute. After fine-tuning, attention changes mostly in the late layers, where the answer position concentrates on the entity names.

Cleaning applies NFC/NFKC normalisation, keeps only characters of the language's script block, repairs OCR damage (detached matras, broken conjuncts, detached Assamese suffixes), drops short or mostly non-script documents, and removes exact (SHA-256) and near (MinHash, 0.6) duplicates. % ============================ PHASE 2 ============================
\section{Phase 2: pretraining}

Both architectures have 8 layers, $d_{\text{model}}=384$, 6 heads, a 512-token context and tied embeddings (V1: 24,982,272 parameters; V2: 25,172,352). Each was trained for 15,259 AdamW steps of 32,768 tokens (500M tokens, about 0.7 of an epoch) with a cosine schedule from $6\times10^{-4}$ to $6\times10^{-5}$.

% ============================ PHASE 3 ============================
\section{Phase 3: relational reasoning}

\subsection{Data and fine-tuning}

Synthetic examples state comparisons between named people on one attribute (age, height, savings) and ask about one pair; answers come from a symbolic solver. Train, validation and test names are disjoint, premises are shuffled and stated in random direction, the question's relation word is drawn independently of the answer, and no training query exceeds 3 hops. Models are fine-tuned with a target-only loss, either on the answer (Direct) or on a rationale followed by the answer (CoT), and checkpoints are selected on the relation accuracy of generated validation answers. Five recipes (plain, real-name mix, dropout, short-query curriculum, CoT warm-up) were run with two seeds each: 44 runs.

\noindent\textbf{Is it reasoning?} Names and prompts never leak between splits, but 90\% of main-test premise structures also occur in training, and answering with the most common training answer for a structure scores 0.95. On 4--10-hop, branch and redundant splits no structure occurs in training and this lookup is at chance, yet Hindi Direct models score 0.82--0.99 there: they apply the relation to chains they have never seen.

\noindent\textbf{Weaknesses.} Premises about another attribute lower Hindi Direct accuracy from 0.95--0.99 on plain 3-hop chains to 0.73, although the answers name the right pair. On 10-hop chains the weaker models' answers stop naming the queried pair (Hindi V1 CoT names it in 11\% of answers), which is a failure to carry the chain through generation.

\subsection{Attention before and after fine-tuning}

Fine-tuning leaves layer-0 attention nearly unchanged (JSD 0.03--0.05) and changes layers 5--7 several times more (Figure~\ref{fig:attn}). In Direct models the last prompt token, where the answer begins, moves from 0.31--0.55 to 0.72--0.99 of its final-layer attention on entity names; CoT models shift less.

% ============================ QUESTIONS ============================
\section{The four comparison questions}

\subsection{Q1. How did data scale and quality differ between Model H and Model L?}

Both training sets hold about 715M tokens with their own tokenizers, and both models saw 500M of them. Hindi relies more on large published corpora (Sangraha, OSCAR, CC-100) and crawled literature archives; Assamese more on project crawlers of regional news sites and textbook PDFs. The cleaning pipeline was identical, with extra OCR repairs for Assamese suffixes.

\subsection{Q2. How do language-modelling and reasoning results compare across the two tiers?}

Assamese has higher token perplexity (51.6 vs 36.2) but lower bits per byte (0.457 vs 0.477), so the two models compress their languages comparably. Reasoning is where the lower-resource language falls behind: 0.833 vs 0.961 on the test set, 0.296 vs 0.846 at 10 hops for V1 Direct, with larger seed variance. A CoT warm-up raised Assamese V1 from 0.655 to 0.831.

\subsection{Q3. Which tokenizer and corpus factors most affected the lower-resource model?}

Assamese fertility is 12\% higher and names inflect by suffixation, so the same entity appears as different token sequences; copying names is a large part of the task (Assamese V2 Direct names the queried pair in 82\% of answers, Hindi V1 in 100\%). Keeping only script characters also removed ASCII punctuation, so the question mark is a byte-fallback token in both languages.

\subsection{Q4. What evidence explains the observed differences?}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Deeper, structurally new splits show generalisation where a lookup fails.
  \item CoT accuracy falls steadily with depth while Direct stays above 0.84 for Hindi V1: a written rationale compounds errors.
  \item V2 has the better language model but learns the task less well and copies names less reliably; we hypothesise, without testing it here, that V1's learned absolute positions suit these short templated prompts.
  \item Fine-tuning re-wires the late layers to attend to the entities the answer depends on.
  \item Attribute binding is the shared weakness and the natural next step: training data with mixed-attribute premises.
\end{itemize}
